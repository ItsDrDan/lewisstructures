Lewis structure recipe
  1. Count valence electrons. Add electrons for a negative charge; subtract for a positive charge.
  2. Choose the skeleton. H is terminal; the less electronegative atom is often central.
  3. Add single bonds, using two electrons per bond.
  4. Complete terminal octets, then place remaining electrons on central atoms.
  5. Make multiple bonds if needed. Check the electron budget and formal charges.

Bond & molecular polarity

Use the electronegativities from your yellow sheet. For this game's classification convention:

  • ΔEN ≤ 0.4: nonpolar covalent
  • ΔEN > 0.4: polar covalent for the molecular bonds in this practice bank.

The cutoff is an approximate teaching guideline. A large ΔEN can suggest ionic character, but it does not by itself make a substance ionic: HF and BF₃ are covalent molecules. “Nonpolar” at the cutoff means approximately nonpolar.

Bond dipoles point toward the higher EN atom. Use the 3D molecular geometry to decide if the dipoles cancel. CO₂ has polar bonds but is a nonpolar molecule.

Octet exceptions (CHEM 1A)
  • H: a duet (2 electrons).
  • Electron-deficient: Be often has 4; B often has 6.
  • Odd-electron species: NO and NO₂ contain an unpaired electron.
  • Expanded valence: period 3 and heavier central atoms can be drawn with more than 8 electrons in introductory Lewis structures.

C, N, O, and F do not expand their octets.

Resonance (CHEM 1A)

Keep the atoms and their connections fixed. Move electron pairs and change bond orders. The real species is a resonance hybrid; it does not switch between separate structures.

In this mode, build and save two different equivalent contributors. Fixed atom labels let the checker distinguish which bond carries the multiple bond.
